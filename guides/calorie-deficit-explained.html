Calorie Deficit Explained — How Weight Loss Actually Works

What a calorie deficit is, how to set one safely, and why steady deficits beat crash diets.

The basic idea

Weight loss comes down to energy balance. Your body burns a certain number of calories each day to keep you alive, move, and digest food. If you eat fewer calories than you burn, your body makes up the difference from stored energy, mostly body fat. That gap is called a calorie deficit. It sounds simple, and the principle is simple, but putting it into practice takes planning, because hunger, habits, social eating, and daily life all push against it. A deficit of about 500 calories a day typically leads to around 0.5 kg of fat loss per week, since a kilogram of body fat stores roughly 7,700 calories.

Finding your maintenance calories

Before you can set a deficit, you need to know roughly how many calories you burn. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates your basal metabolic rate, the calories you burn at rest, from your weight, height, age, and sex, and multiplies it by an activity factor to give your total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE. Our calculator on the home page does this for you. The result is an estimate, not a measurement. Two people with the same numbers can burn different amounts, so treat the TDEE as a starting point, follow it for two or three weeks, and adjust based on what your weight actually does.

Choosing the size of your deficit

A deficit of 300 to 500 calories a day suits most people. It is large enough to see progress and small enough to live with. A deficit of 750 to 1,000 calories a day can work for people with more weight to lose, but it brings more hunger and a higher risk of giving up. Very large deficits, especially below 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men, should only be done with medical supervision, because they can cause nutrient shortfalls, muscle loss, and fatigue. A smaller deficit that you can keep for six months beats a large one you abandon after two weeks.

How to create the deficit

You can create a deficit by eating less, moving more, or both, and a mix usually works best. Eating less is the bigger lever, because it is hard to out-exercise a large surplus. Start by cutting the easiest calories: sugary drinks, alcohol, large portions of refined starch, and frequent snacking. Add movement you enjoy, such as walking, cycling, or swimming, rather than exercise you hate. Track your food for a week or two to learn where your calories actually come from; most people are surprised by at least one item. Awareness alone often removes several hundred calories a day.

What about metabolism slowing down?

As you lose weight, your body burns fewer calories, because a smaller body needs less energy. This is called metabolic adaptation, and it is normal. It does not mean your metabolism is broken. It means your deficit shrinks over time, so you may need to eat a little less or move a little more to keep losing. Strength training helps, because it preserves muscle, and muscle burns more calories than fat. Protein also helps, because it keeps you full and protects muscle. Plateaus are part of the process, not a sign that the deficit has stopped working forever.

Making it sustainable

A deficit works only if you can live with it. Build meals around protein, fibre, and vegetables, which keep you full for fewer calories. Keep some foods you enjoy in the plan, in smaller portions, rather than banning them and craving them later. Plan for social events instead of pretending they will not happen. Weigh yourself a few times a week under the same conditions and watch the average, not single readings. And remember that weight loss is not linear: water, salt, hormones, and food volume make the scale jump around. Judge progress over weeks, not days.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

Start with your TDEE minus 300 to 500 calories. Use the calculator on our home page, follow the target for two to three weeks, then adjust up or down based on your actual weight trend.

Is a 1,000-calorie deficit too much?

For many people, yes. It can cause strong hunger and fatigue. A 500-calorie deficit is a safer starting point. Larger deficits should be discussed with a doctor, especially if you have a medical condition.

Why am I not losing weight in a deficit?

Common reasons are underestimating portions, forgetting drinks and snacks, a TDEE estimate that was too high, or normal water-weight swings. Track honestly for two weeks and check the trend before changing the plan.
